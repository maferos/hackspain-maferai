> The paper generalizes convolutional networks to group convolutions equivariant to rotations and reflections, raising accuracy on rotated MNIST and CIFAR10 at a roughly fixed parameter count.

## Abstract

We introduce Group equivariant Convolutional Neural Networks (G-CNNs), a natural generalization of convolutional neural networks that reduces sample complexity by exploiting symmetries. G-CNNs use G-convolutions, a new type of layer that enjoys a substantially higher degree of weight sharing than regular convolution layers. G-convolutions increase the expressive capacity of the network without increasing the number of parameters. Group convolution layers are easy to use and can be implemented with negligible computational overhead for discrete groups generated by translations, reflections and rotations. G-CNNs achieve state of the art results on CIFAR10 and rotated MNIST. (arXiv)

## 🧠 Key ideas (atomic)

- G-CNNs generalize convolutional networks to exploit larger groups of symmetries, including rotations and reflections, not only translations. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 1 ^cohen2016group-001
- Convolutional weight sharing is effective because the label function and data distribution in most perception tasks are approximately invariant to shifts. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 1 ^cohen2016group-002
- Standard convolution layers are translation equivariant, so the translation symmetry is preserved by each layer and can be exploited in higher layers. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 1 ^cohen2016group-003
- G-CNN representations have the structure of a linear G-space, where each vector carries a pose that group elements can transform. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-004
- A G-CNN filter detects co-occurrences of features with a preferred relative pose and matches them in every global pose via G-convolution. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-005
- Invariance is a special kind of equivariance in which the output transformation operator is the identity for every group element. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-006
- The authors argue general equivariance is more useful than invariance, since invariant features cannot show whether they are in the right spatial configuration. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-007
- Equivariance to symmetry transformations constrains the network in a way that can aid generalization, preserving the sameness of inputs under symmetry transformations. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-008
- Lenc and Vedaldi showed that AlexNet trained on ImageNet spontaneously learns representations that are equivariant to flips, scaling and rotation. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:cited` p. 2 ^cohen2016group-009
- The group p4 consists of all compositions of translations and rotations by 90 degrees about any center of rotation in a square grid. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 3 ^cohen2016group-010
- The group p4m consists of all compositions of translations, mirror reflections and 90-degree rotations about any center of rotation in the grid. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 3 ^cohen2016group-011
- Under a 90-degree rotation of a p4 function, each planar patch moves to the next rotation coordinate and simultaneously rotates by 90 degrees. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 4 ^cohen2016group-012
- A p4m function has 8 planar patches, each associated with a mirroring and a rotation, visualised with rotation arrows and reflection lines. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 4 ^cohen2016group-013
- Planar correlation is shown to be equivariant to translations: a translation followed by a correlation equals a correlation followed by a translation. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-014
- Planar convolutions are not equivariant to rotations: correlating a rotated image with a filter equals rotating the correlation with the inverse-rotated filter. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-015
- If an ordinary CNN learns rotated copies of the same filter, its stack of feature maps is equivariant, although individual feature maps are not. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-016
- The first-layer G-correlation replaces the filter shift by a transformation from a group G, so its output feature map is a function on G. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-017
- The full G-correlation between functions on G is derived to be equivariant, in complete analogy to the planar translation case. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-018
- If G is not commutative, G-correlation is not commutative, but the two argument orders are related by the involution of the feature map. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-019
- Bias terms can be added to G-conv layers as long as there is only one bias per G-feature map. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-020
- Batch normalization should be implemented with a single scale and bias parameter per G-feature map in order to preserve equivariance. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-021
- Since the sum of two G-equivariant feature maps is also G-equivariant, G-conv layers can be used in highway and residual networks. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 5 ^cohen2016group-022
- Pointwise nonlinearities commute with the left transformation operator, so rectified feature maps inherit the transformation properties of the previous layer. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-023
- Non-strided max-pooling over a transformed pooling domain commutes with the group action, as derived in the supplementary material. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-024
- In a G-CNN, stride is generalized by subsampling on a subgroup H, giving feature maps equivariant to H but not G. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-025
- Pooling over the cosets of a subgroup H yields a feature map invariant to the right action of H, a function on G/H. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:computed` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-026
- Since all layer types are equivariant, the authors expect G-conv parameter sharing to be effective at arbitrary depth in stacked networks. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-027
- For split groups such as p4 and p4m, G-correlation is computed by transforming the filters and then calling a fast planar correlation routine. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-028
- The G-convolution costs roughly as much as a planar convolution with the augmented filter bank, because the filter transformation cost is negligible. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 6 ^cohen2016group-029
- The filter transformation is a permutation of filter entries, implemented by a GPU kernel that looks up precomputed indices for each output cell. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-030
- The G-convolution for a new group can be added by simply implementing a map from array indices to group-element matrices. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-031
- The rotated MNIST dataset contains 62000 randomly rotated handwritten digits, split into training, validation and test sets of 10000, 2000 and 50000. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-032
- The Z2CNN baseline has 7 layers of 3 × 3 convolutions with 20 channels each, relu, batch normalization, dropout and max-pooling after layer 2. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-033
- The Z2CNN baseline obtained 5.03 ± 0.0020 test error on rotated MNIST, short of the 3.98 previous state of the art by Schmidt and Roth. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-034
- The P4CNN replaced each convolution by a p4-convolution and divided the number of filters by 2 to keep parameters approximately fixed. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-035
- The P4CNN added max-pooling over rotations after the last convolution layer and was trained without dropout, which it performed better without. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-036
- The P4CNN almost halves the error rate of the previous state of the art on rotated MNIST, with 2.28% versus 3.98% error. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-037
- P4CNNRotationPooling, which applies coset max-pooling over rotations after every p4-convolution, reached 3.21 ± 0.0012 test error on rotated MNIST. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-038
- Testing whether premature invariance is undesirable, P4CNNRotationPooling performed significantly worse than P4CNN, which does not pool over rotations in intermediate layers. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-039
- The CIFAR-10 dataset of 60k 32 × 32 images in 10 classes was split into 40k training, 10k validation and 10k test images. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-040
- The baselines were the All-CNN-C architecture of 9 convolution layers and a wide 44-layer residual network called ResNet44. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 7 ^cohen2016group-041
- To keep parameter counts approximately invariant, the number of filters was halved in p4-conv layers and divided by roughly 3 in p4m-conv layers. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-042
- The augmented CIFAR10+ setting denotes moderate data augmentation with horizontal flips and small translations, following Goodfellow et al. and others. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:reported` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-043

- On augmented CIFAR10+, p4m-ResNet44 reached 4.94 test error compared with 5.61 for the planar Z2 ResNet44. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-049
- The authors caution that radical differences in model sizes and architectures make it difficult to infer the intrinsic merit of the compared techniques. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-050
- A 26-layer wide ResNet trained with moderate augmentation reached 4.19% error with p4m convolutions versus 5.27% with planar convolutions. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-051
- The 4.19% p4m result is comparable to the 4.17% error of Zagoruyko and Komodakis while using 7.2M versus 36.5M parameters. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-052
- The authors conclude that p4 and p4m convolution layers can serve as a drop-in replacement for standard convolutions that consistently improves results. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-053
- G-CNNs benefit from data augmentation in the same way as convolutional networks, as long as the augmentation comes from a group larger than G. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-054
- Although CIFAR objects typically appear upright, G-convolutions gave substantial accuracy increases, indicating that a full symmetry need not exist for benefit. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:measured` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-055
- The authors plan G-CNNs on hexagonal lattices, which have more symmetries than square grids, and G-CNNs for 3D space groups. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-056
- One limitation the authors state is that the method as presented here only works for discrete groups of transformations. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-057
- Convolution on continuous locally compact groups is mathematically well-defined but may be hard to approximate in an equivariant manner. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-058
- A full enumeration of transformations may not be feasible if the group is large, a challenge already identified by Gens and Domingos. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:cited` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-059
- The authors believe adding mathematical structure to representations could enhance the ability of neural nets to see abstract similarities between superficially different concepts. (Cohen & Welling, 2016) `ev:asserted` p. 8 ^cohen2016group-060

## 📖 Glossary

- **Equivariance** — A map commutes with a transformation: transform-then-map equals map-then-transform.
- **G-convolution** — Convolution where filters are transformed by elements of a group G, not only shifted.
- **p4** — Group of translations and 90-degree rotations on the square pixel grid.
- **p4m** — Group of translations, mirror reflections and 90-degree rotations on the square grid.
- **Coset pooling** — Pooling over cosets gH of a subgroup H, giving features invariant to H.
- **Split group** — Group whose elements decompose into a translation and an origin-fixing transformation.
- **Involution** — Feature-map operation f*(g) = f(g^-1); for planar maps a point reflection.

## ❓ Open questions

- How can group convolutions be approximated equivariantly for continuous (locally compact) groups such as all rotations?
- How can G-CNNs scale to large groups where full enumeration of transformations is infeasible?
- Do the CIFAR10 gains persist when G-convolutions are combined with larger networks, extreme augmentation or ensembles that the compared methods might use?
- How do G-CNNs perform on hexagonal lattices and 3D space groups, which the authors propose as future work?

The abstract's claim of reduced sample complexity is not directly measured in the body.

## Suggested new concepts

- Group equivariant convolution — core operation generalizing weight sharing to symmetry groups; foundational for equivariant deep learning.
- Equivariance vs invariance — the paper's argument against premature invariance recurs across geometric deep learning.
- Coset pooling — a general mechanism for obtaining invariance to a subgroup while keeping equivariance elsewhere.
- Wallpaper groups p4 and p4m — the concrete discrete symmetry groups used to build rotation- and reflection-equivariant CNNs.
